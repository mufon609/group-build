Is that text a scam?

A message arrives. Before you tap, we investigate who really sent it.

The problem

Smishing works because the text looks like it's from someone you trust.

Package held

"USPS: confirm your address within 12 hours"

Unpaid toll

"E-ZPass Final Notice: pay $6.99 to avoid a $50 fee"

Bank locked

"Chase Alert: verify your identity immediately"

Refund bait

"IRS: you are eligible for a $1,400 refund"

Our idea

Don't just read the words. Check the claim.

  • Every scam text names a company to borrow its trust.
  • So we ask one sharp question: does that company actually own this sender?
  • Real brands text from published short codes. Scammers use random 10-digit lines, foreign numbers, or email addresses.
  • Some brands, like USPS, the IRS, and E-ZPass, never initiate texts like this at all.

How it works

Five checks, all offline, in under a millisecond.

  • 1Classify the sender. Short code, 10-digit number, email, or contact name.
  • 2Find the claimed company. 20 most-impersonated brands in a local knowledge base.
  • 3Ownership check. Confirmed · Unverified · Suspicious · Contradicted
  • 4Inspect links. Lookalike domains, shorteners, risky TLDs, bare IPs.
  • 5Read the wording. Urgency, suspension threats, "Reply Y then reopen the link".

Score → Safe · Suspicious · Likely Scam, with plain-language reasons and what to do next.

Same brand, two senders

The ownership check is what separates them.

Built to plug in

Two script tags. One function.

<script src="scam/brands.js"></script>
<script src="scam/scam.js"></script>

const r = investigate(sender, text);
r.risk_level          // "Safe" | "Suspicious" | "Likely Scam"
r.company_ownership   // [{ brand, status, reason }]
r.urls                // [{ host, flags }]
r.recommended_action  // "Don't tap. Forward to 7726..."

Reads the team's text-log format directly. No server, no API key, works offline. Python twin produces a Markdown report; Claude adds an analyst write-up when a key is present.
